    Keynote Talks
    WASP @ IJCNLP-AACL 2026

    WASP 2026 is delighted to welcome the following keynote speakers.

    Jessy Li

    The University of Texas at Austin

    TalkSalience and Inquiry in Scientific Literature

    Abstract

    In this talk, we view the processing of scientific literature as an (implicitly) interactive discourse process: the reader is constantly abstracting out the most salient information, while staying inquisitive about the content. I will discuss the role of questions, first in deriving information salience from research documents. Using summarization as a behavioral probe, we reveal that across different lengths of summaries, the questions that models tend to answer remain intriguingly consistent. Next, we show how questions play important roles in connecting different pieces of a scientific document via generative discourse models, and extend existing frameworks to multimodal settings, modeling the connections across passages and figures. Finally, I will end the talk with brief discussions on AI review pilots at recent large conferences.

    Bio

    Jessy Li is an associate professor in the Linguistics Department and (by courtesy) the Computer Science Department at the University of Texas at Austin. She received her Ph.D. from the Department of Computer and Information Science at the University of Pennsylvania in 2017. Her research interests are in computational linguistics and natural language processing, specifically discourse and pragmatics, generation and evaluation, and language & code. She is a recipient of an NSF CAREER Award, ACL and EMNLP Outstanding Paper Awards, an ACM SIGSOFT Distinguished Paper Award, among others. Jessy is an AI research lead at the NSF-Simons CosmicAI Institute. She is also the Secretary of NAACL, and a Program Chair of the EMNLP 2026 conference.

    Pavlos Protopapas

    Harvard University

    TalkFrom General-Purpose LLMs to Grounded Tools for Scientific Discovery

    Abstract

    Large language models offer new ways to search, interpret, and synthesize scientific literature. Yet their responses can reflect biases in their training data, uneven coverage of research, and claims that are difficult to verify. How can we make these models useful while retaining control over the evidence and reasoning behind their outputs? Drawing on two recent research projects, I will discuss building applications on top of NASA's Astrophysics Data System (ADS) that connect language models to scholarly sources and guide their use through explicit workflows. The talk will explore how retrieval, tool use, and verification can help make outputs more transparent and assessable, while examining the limitations that remain. The central question is how to design AI tools that support scientific judgment and allow researchers to inspect and challenge their results.

    Bio

    Pavlos Protopapas is the Scientific Program Director for the master's programs in data science and computational science engineering at the Harvard John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences. As an educator, he currently teaches the CS109 course series for basic and advanced data science and a course in Machine Learning Operations at Harvard University. He previously taught the capstone courses for the master's programs, and also a course in deep reinforcement learning. His courses are also available through the Harvard Extension School and on HarvardX. In addition he has participated in data science schools around the world in Chile, Colombia, India, and Rwanda. Pavlos has a PhD in theoretical physics from the University of Pennsylvania and his research interests are at the intersection of astronomy, machine learning, and statistics.
